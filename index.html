What am I looking at?

You are looking down on the Moon's south pole. The side facing Earth is at the top. Each numbered dot is a place NASA is studying for an astronaut landing. Tap a dot (or a name in the list) to choose it. The horizon view below then shows the sky from that place: the Sun (yellow) and the Earth (blue). Press Play to watch them move.

What am I looking at?

Imagine standing at the site and turning all the way around: this is that full circle, unrolled flat. The grey band is the ground. The yellow dot is the Sun and the blue dot is the Earth; a hollow dot means it is hidden below the horizon. Dotted lines show where each one travels during the 30 days of the slider. Near the pole the Sun never climbs high — it circles the horizon about once a month.

About this tool

The Lunar Landing Site Navigator helps mission planners, educators and the public compare landing sites near the Moon's south pole by showing where the Sun and the Earth are relative to the horizon.

Honest limitations (work in progress)

  • Site positions are approximate. NASA published the candidate regions as named areas about 15 km across; our markers are placed near their centres and labelled “approximate”.
  • Crater outlines are schematic circles from catalogue positions and sizes, not imagery.
  • Flat horizon. Real mountains and crater rims are not modelled yet. You can raise or lower the “local horizon height” to explore their effect.
  • Map lighting is for a smooth ball. The day/night shading on the map ignores terrain shadows.
  • Sun and Earth sizes in the horizon view are drawn slightly enlarged so they are easy to see and tap.

How positions are calculated

Sun and Moon positions come from the open-source astronomy-engine library (checked by its author against NASA JPL data). The Moon's tilt and wobble (libration) use the International Astronomical Union rotation model. Times are UTC.
