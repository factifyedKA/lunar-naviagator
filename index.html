About the Lunar Landing Site Navigator

Built for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, challenge “Lunar Landing Site Navigator”.

Purpose

Missions to the Moon’s south pole live or die by two things: sunlight for power and a clear view of the Earth for direct radio contact. Both depend on the place, the date and the landscape. This tool lets mission planners, educators and the public compare landing sites and dates in seconds, with no installation and no login.

How it works

  1. Where are the Sun and the Earth? Positions of the Sun and the Moon come from the open-source astronomy-engine library, which its author verifies against NASA JPL data.
  2. How is the Moon turned? The Moon’s tilt, spin and monthly wobble (libration) follow the rotation model of the International Astronomical Union (IAU).
  3. What does the site see? For a site’s latitude and longitude we work out how high the Sun and the Earth are above the horizon (elevation) and in which compass direction (azimuth).
  4. What does the landscape hide? A horizon mask made from NASA LRO/LOLA elevation data says how high the real terrain rises in each of 360 directions. The Sun or the Earth counts as visible only above that skyline.
  5. Add it up. Stepping through time hour by hour gives the timelines, the five key numbers, the overall score and the best landing windows.

Everything is calculated in your browser. The heavy work runs in a background worker so the page stays smooth. All times are UTC.

NASA data and tools used

  • LRO/LOLA elevation model LDEM_75S_240M (data set LRO-L-LOLA-4-GDR-V1.0, LOLA team, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, via the NASA PDS Geosciences Node): the terrain horizons of all sites, the 960 m grid for custom points and the shaded-relief map.
  • LOLA average illumination and Earth-visibility maps AVGVISIB_65S_240M_201608 (LOLA team, NASA PDS): reference values on the Validation page.
  • NASA JPL Horizons System: reference Sun and Earth positions on the Validation page.
  • NASA Artemis III candidate landing regions (NASA news release, 28 October 2024, and the earlier list of 19 August 2022): the names of the regions shown.

Other tools and sources

  • astronomy-engine v2.1.19 by Don Cross (MIT licence), included in this site’s files.
  • IAU Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements: lunar rotation model.
  • IAU Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature (USGS): crater names; positions are rounded.
  • Okabe–Ito colour-blind-safe palette.
  • No other libraries, no trackers, no cookies, no external requests.

Honest limitations

  • Site positions are approximate. NASA published the candidate regions as named areas about 15 km across, without centre coordinates in the announcement. Our markers are placed near the named features and are labelled “approximate”; three (Peak near Cabeus B, Mons Mouton Plateau, Slater Plain) could be off by about 1°.
  • Terrain is real but coarse. Horizons come from a 240 m grid (960 m for points you place yourself). Boulders, small craters and anything finer than the grid are not seen. The observer stands 2 m above the highest ground within 5 km of each marker (1 km for the reference points).
  • Flat mode ignores terrain completely; it is there for comparison and teaching.
  • Moon orientation uses the IAU series, which follows NASA’s high-precision lunar frame to a few thousandths of a degree (see Validation).
  • Map lighting is for a smooth ball. The day/night shading on the map ignores terrain shadows; the relief picture uses artificial lighting from the upper left.
  • Sun and Earth sizes in the horizon view are drawn slightly enlarged so they are easy to see and tap. Eclipses by the Earth are handled approximately.
  • Radio link means only “the Earth is above the local horizon by your chosen margin”. It does not model ground stations, antennas or signal strength.
  • Scores are a planning aid, not a mission decision: they ignore slopes, temperature, landing hazards and science value.
  • Time step is one hour. Events shorter than that can be missed in timelines.

Use of AI

Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, was used to help write the code, tests and documentation of this project. The team made the decisions, tested the tool and checked the results. The numbers shown come from the calculations and NASA data described above, not from an AI; the Validation page tests them against NASA references every time it is opened.
